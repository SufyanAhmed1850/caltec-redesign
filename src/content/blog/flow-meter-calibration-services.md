A flow meter decides how much material goes into a batch and how much product leaves the site. When it drifts, the error rarely announces itself — it shows up months later as a yield gap, a reconciliation problem or an audit finding.

Most flow calibration problems we see are not caused by the calibration itself. They are caused by what was — or wasn't — checked beforehand. These are the seven things our technicians confirm before any reference is connected.

## 1. Meter technology and process fluid

Electromagnetic, turbine, vortex, thermal mass and Coriolis meters all respond differently, and each needs its own method. Confirm the fluid too: water, steam, gas and aggressive chemicals change what a valid test setup looks like.

## 2. Traceable reference standards

The reference is only useful if its own calibration chain is intact. Check that its certificate is current and traceable to a recognised national or accredited laboratory.

## 3. Ambient conditions

Temperature, humidity and pressure all influence the result, especially in pharmaceutical and food environments where tolerances are tight. Record them alongside your readings.

## 4. Installation and orientation

Straight-run lengths, alignment and upstream disturbances matter. A meter installed badly will be calibrated badly — no adjustment compensates for turbulent inlet conditions.

## 5. Zero-flow baseline

With the line isolated and no flow present, the meter should read zero. Establishing that baseline first makes every later reading meaningful.

## 6. Documentation ready to go

Prepare the record before you begin: as-found readings, reference values, as-left readings and the tolerance you are working to. A clean record is what your auditor will actually look at.

## 7. Isolation and safety

Depressurise and isolate as required. High-pressure and hazardous lines need a permit-to-work mindset, not a quick valve turn.

> Calibration isn't about adjusting numbers. It's about proving a reading can be trusted.
